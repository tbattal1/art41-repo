**Pilot role:** stress test of the lock-in end of the configuration space.
**Governance pattern:** state-operator port inside a vertically integrated state-owned group — TNPA is a division of Transnet SOC Ltd (no separate legal personality despite a 2005 statutory corporatization order); container handling by sister division TPT; highest compound lock-in and highest de jure social-value integration in the pilot set.

## Structural summary
- TNPA (Transnet National Ports Authority) is a DIVISION of Transnet SOC Ltd — no independent legal personality; Transnet is 100 % state-owned (Department of Public Enterprises, DPE).
- National Ports Act 2005, s. 3(2), ORDERS TNPA to be established "immediately" as a separate company; still not implemented in 2019 ("persistently defied legislation").
- Tariffs: TNPA sets tariffs BUT approval by the Ports Regulator is mandatory (Act s. 72(1)(a)); bound by the 2014 tariff methodology.
- Investment decisions sit inside the Transnet group balance sheet; no independent board (until 2021).
- Container handling: TPT (Transnet Port Terminals, a division of the same group) handles ~100 % of containers; Durban carries ~65 % of the country's container volume. Formal landlord structure (TPT leases from TNPA) but INTRA-GROUP — functionally close to a public service port.
- Private participation exists in bulk/liquid cargo (TPT handles ~30 % of bulk → ~70 % private bulk).
- Outside the window: 2021 Ramaphosa announcement of an independent subsidiary (Ş2 robustness); ICTSI–Pier 2 partnership (2023 agreement, union resistance) → Ş2-period turbulence; October 2022 force-majeure strike.

## C4 — Compound lock-in → **0.89**

**(a) Regulatory rigidity (window 2005–2019) → 1**
The 2005 Act was enacted at the start of the window — but the real issue is that the Act's CORE provision (corporatization) has gone UNIMPLEMENTED for 14+ years. This is a heavier category than a "failed reform attempt": **a reform enacted into law but de facto blocked** ("Transnet has made very little progress... despite the legislation", "persistently defied legislation").
**Coder's note (codebook revision note #9, resolved as K6):** a third category was added to the v2 C4a table: "enacted-but-unimplemented core reform" = 1.0 (the heaviest rigidity: lock-in at the level of the rule of law). Approved by the author (K6); the codebook row reads "Enacted-but-unimplemented core reform (Durban: 2005 Act corporatization order, 16 years — K6 approved) = 1". Mersin's "framework act blocked at draft stage" stays at 0.67 — the gradation is consistent. Durban C4a = 1.

**(b) Financial lock-in → 1** (Branch B — state-operator port; SOE financial constraint, 3/3 indicators)
The v1 rule measured concession term → Durban has no long private concession → the rule would have returned 0. That would have been absurd: Durban's financial lock-in is not a concession but an **SOE balance-sheet crisis** — Transnet's debt burden (2013 BRICS USD 5 billion loan, dollar-denominated debt), a documented investment gap, and ageing equipment.
**Coder's note (codebook revision note #10b, resolved as K7):** C4b became two-branched in v2: **Branch A (concessioned/leased ports):** remaining-term bands (existing rule). **Branch B (state-operator ports):** SOE financial constraint — three indicators: documented investment gap / debt-service pressure / equipment age–renewal gap (0 = 0, 1 = 0.33, 2 = 0.67, 3 = 1). Branch choice is justified on the coding sheet; in mixed structures the dominant structure governs. Approved by the author (K7). Durban under Branch B: 3/3 (documented at presidential level: "lack of investment, outdated equipment, understaffed") → 1. Codebook precedent: "Durban: 3/3 → 1".

**(c) Social-legitimacy gap (window 2015–2019) → 0.67**
TWO documented dispute clusters in the 2015–2019 window:
1. **SDCEA campaign cluster:** sustained organized opposition to the dig-out port + Clairwood Logistics Park — 2015 Clairwood objection/appeal, continuing protests, 2018 protest against the BRICS/NDB loan; the dig-out port was postponed in 2016 to 2032. Sources: SDCEA (2015), Contested Ports, EJAtlas, Pambazuka (2018). Under K12 this counts as operationally effective: the SDCEA opposition caused the dig-out postponement and was accompanied by a strike threat (codebook precedent for K12).
2. **State capture / legitimacy crisis:** PARI 2018 report — "governance structures at Transnet were repurposed to ENABLE large-scale corruption and rent-seeking"; the Zondo Commission process. Source: Springer (2020, citing PARI 2018).
(The 2019 strike threat was averted at the last minute — no strike of ≥3 days occurred, not counted. The October 2022 force-majeure strike is outside the window; it enters the Ş2 robustness coding.)
2 clusters = 0.67.

**C4 composite:** (1 + 1 + 0.67)/3 = **0.89** → the lock-in end anticipated by the design is empirically confirmed.
**Coder's note:** the pilot sheet gave the range 0.78–0.89, the lower bound corresponding to C4a = 0.67 if the K6 category were not adopted. With K6 and K7 approved, C4a = 1 and C4b = 1 → 0.89, as recorded in the verification addendum's summary rows.

**Case finding (Not#11, "analytical gold"):** Durban is, on paper, the most socially integrated port in the pilot set (C5 = 0.95) and in the field a legitimacy crisis (C4c = 0.67 + state capture). Precise supporting evidence: by the NPCC's own statement, TNPA "to its knowledge NEVER submitted" its tariff applications to the NPCC despite the legal obligation (NPCC 2018, cited in Springer) — the body exists, meets and keeps minutes; the authority bypasses it. A case in which the de jure/de facto divergence is documented this cleanly is rare. This is the central example for the Discussion. C5 measures de jure institutionalization, C4c de facto legitimacy; their divergence is not a contradiction but a FINDING — fsQCA already separates the two as distinct conditions. Rotterdam is the candidate for the opposite profile (low de jure, relatively high de facto).
